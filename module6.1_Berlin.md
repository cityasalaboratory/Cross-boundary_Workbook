# Getting there
Around the table (Figure 1), many people were heading somewhere new: a business, a PhD, a job. So was I. I'm reorienting myself professionally, and over the years I have collected a lot of diverse educational and professional experiences: ethnology, psychology, business, design, corporate innovation, consulting and, most recently, applied research. I often say that having so many different interests sometimes feel like a liability. It's hard to focus only on one field when there are so many, and a patchwork CV often doesn't cover all the requirements in a job ad while leaving many other experiences and skills irrelevant. So, when I read about the CAL:TS project and this masterclass on their website one lazy Friday afternoon, I almost felt understood. The description promised: 

<blockquote class="case-quote">
"Participants learn how to initiate and run collaborative enquiries with universities, civic actors and users, and how to route results back into organisational decision making."
</blockquote>

I mean, for a (wannabe) world-saving Millennial who's slightly lost in our chaotic times, this sounds ideal. So, I signed up hoping to explore transdisciplinary in a more structured way, to connect with people who share these in-between interests, and to see whether this could be where my private, academic and professional interests finally align.

# A warehouse full of possibilities
The masterclass took place at MotionLab (Figure 2) and it was kind of the joke of the day that the vibes of the industrial complex where the maker space is located made you want to double-check your kidneys were still there before leaving. Berlin being Berlin. [Flag: keep, soften or cut?] But once you find the beach chairs out front and walk through the towering gate of the maker space, you're in for a treat: a friendly, slightly quirky reception, then an open warehouse with a coffee area, co-working spaces, 3D printers and an old bus repurposed as a place to meet and relax. Thorsten, one of the organisers, was there to welcome me, and I felt comfortable from the first moment. Like everyone else, I immediately had an itch to try out some of the machinery.

# A crowd of generalists
Within the first 30 minutes everyone had four genuinely enjoyable conversations (Figure 3), and I arrived late at the first session because of one of them. What hit home first was the feeling of coming home, intellectually speaking. The mixed backgrounds spanned science communication, music, anthropology, business development, startup leadership, design engineering, circular economy and fundraising, and they had all already done so much that I almost felt out of place. Luckily, it was a self-selected and inviting crowd: most of us work in fields driving socio-ecological sustainability, many have moved between disciplines, sectors and roles, and there is a shared desire to organise collaborations and change existing systems in one way or another. Everything seemed to be seeking a way to address complex real-life challenges. And not everyone came for their day job, either. One participant is planning a music festival accompanied by transdisciplinary research. For me, it might become a photography project and hopefully a new career path. 

# Through and beyond disciplines
I think definitions are foundational to any conversation, so I appreciated starting by going deeper into the histories and facets of trans disciplinarity. Two translations for "trans" struck me as simple and impactful: "through" and "beyond". Trans disciplinarity shifts the focus and starts from relevant real-world challenges. It brings discipline to that end but doesn't just add them side by side. Rather, it goes through and beyond disciplines, filling the spaces between them. For many of us, that in-between space, where people and perspectives connect, excites us the most. 

# Let it flow, then filter
After the basics, we explored five methods: citizen science, real-world and living labs, user-driven innovation, storytelling and service learning. They worked as prompts to talk about our own experience, and the conversations went deepest on user-driven innovation (involving users as active innovators), storytelling (as a means to connect, make sense and drive change) and service learning (integrating civic engagement with academic learning).
Personally, I think the storytelling session led by Nina Schmulius was the most fun and useful one. First, we freely wrote for five minutes without a prompt, and judging by the scratchy sounds of pens on paper, nobody was short of things to say. Then we condensed it into an Elfchen, a poem of eleven words. Here is mine where I went from my desire for creativity in the free-writing part to the importance of relationships for that creativity (whatever, okay? don't judge.):

<blockquote class="case-quote">
Relationships
That's it
Creates us all
Whether we know or
Not
</blockquote>

While service learning posted by Jan Kratzer was also very intriguing, next to the speed-dating conversations we covered almost too many that day. I would have loved to go deeper into one of them and apply it to a real case from my own work or life. Others felt similar, so the organisers are putting this feedback into the next edition.

# An honest take
Reflecting on what the description promised, one day gives you a map, a network and a first taste of initiating collaborative enquiries, not the full toolkit to run them, but I wasn't expecting that anyway. The masterclass delivered on all those counts because I left with a bunch of contacts and intriguing resources after a thoroughly enjoyable day (including after hours in a maker space. Again, Berlin being Berlin). I think the masterclass is so valuable, I offered the CAL:TS team my help and am now contributing in a volunteering capacity, which anyone else who's interested can do too. If your many interests sometimes feel more like a liability than a gift and the world seems a bit too complex and overwhelming, go and find your crowd at the next masterclass in Delft. I found mine.
And let's be honest on that front: relationships and methods from events like this often fade quickly, which is why I'm glad the conversation continues in the Knowledge Exchange community on LinkedIn.
