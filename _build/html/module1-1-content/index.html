1.1 Why this Workbook?

An invitation to share knowledge, collaborate, and learn from each other to achieve individual and societal goals. This motivation lies at the heart of the Workbook.

As a citizen of this world, whether affiliated with an academic, government, business, cultural or civil society organisation, you may see yourself in Figure 1, perhaps curious about your role as an agent of change. In this role, you may be concerned with sustainability or survivability challenges.

At one extreme, people with basic comfort and liveability face sustainability challenges, understood as interconnected social, economic, and environmental issues that affect long-term well-being. On the other extreme, those experiencing multiple scarcities (e.g., housing, food, energy, and water) struggle with survival due to limited access to resources. While in some societies the differences between these extremes are greater, both groups can be found worldwide, contributing to environmental degradation for different reasons: the former through overconsumption and waste, and the latter through a lack of technological and economic means to use resources sustainably Halawa, 2024. As a result, each group experiences the consequences of challenges such as climate change, biodiversity loss, migration, urbanisation, and threats to sustainability and democracy in different ways, depending on their circumstances. In this context, one can understand “democracy” (†see glossary) as the meaningful participation of citizens in decision-making, which also implies that responsible citizens actively engage in shaping acceptable standards and inclusive futures for all, regardless of group affiliation ( Ribeiro et al, 2025↗ ). Although values like sustainability and democracy are often framed as universal or long-term goals, this can lead to one group imposing them on others, overlooking cultural differences and failing to acknowledge their own limitations in addressing complex challenges. Accordingly, interventions should not be transferred uncritically between contexts; they require adaptable and flexible design that accounts for evolving values and needs over time ( Davis & Nathan et al 2015↗ ).

Similarly, in the places where you live, study or work, you may observe or personally experience somehow unequal access to basic living standards within and across neighbourhoods and social groups. You may have noticed that tensions between shared and private resources often amplify inequalities and lead to conflicts between identities and competing visions of the future. This awareness may motivate you to imagine more equitable, ethical, and responsible ways of thinking and acting, both individually and collectively. At the same time, you may wonder whether it is possible or worthwhile to address today’s complex challenges without sufficient resources or a clear role in decision-making within your community or organisation. You may also simply not know where to begin or how to make a meaningful difference.

These reflections may lead to a broader question about your own role in addressing such challenges. Hence, do you see yourself as a change- or transition-maker? Keep in mind that “change” (†see glossary) is not only the result of moving from one situation to another which may not always be desired. Change is also an ongoing process that unfolds across different spatial and temporal scales, with or without your involvement ( Mierlo & Beer, 2020↗ ).

When referring to change toward a desired state of sustainability, and to avoid labeling societies as developed or underdeveloped, the term “sustainable development” (†see glossary) has gradually been replaced by “sustainable transitions” (†see glossary) or “transformations”, although these terms do not carry the same meaning. To begin with, a “societal transition” refers to large-scale changes in how society thinks and acts. These transitions can result from abrupt events that disrupt the status quo, prompting groups to respond with solidarity and reinforce existing identities. They may take time to unfold as practices change and identities are reshaped ( Janssen et al., 2022↗ ). To become “sustainable”, transitions should be inclusive or representative to all societal groups . In the process, one must admit that “sustainable transformations” (†see glossary) are transformative visions of the future shaped by whose perspectives and comfort levels define the challenge, as well as by the identities, values and positions of those involved ( Halawa, 2024↗ ). As illustrated in the podcast suggested for the practical exercise of this module ( see below Task 1.2. what does sustainability mean to you? ), researchers from various fields discuss how actions considered sustainable in one place may worsen conditions for other groups of the (world’s) population.

Given this complexity and plurality of perspectives, engaging in societal transitions and transformations whether sustainable or not requires continuous learning and reflection. Therefore, this Workbook is intended to support you in designing the change you seek in your own life. In doing so, you can rethink your role and responsibilities as a transition-maker through a learning journey that encourages you to explore complex challenges and experiment with new ways of thinking and acting, both individually and collectively. To support your learning, we invite you to revisit your personal development plan and compile your work and experiences from the Workbook modules you follow in your own context into a “Learning Journey Portfolio” (†see glossary).

This Workbook further encourages you to go through this journey with a cooperative, and hopeful yet critical attitude:

  • Lasting change takes time. It often begins with you, but it is not only about you. Take time to understand the challenges, imagine ways to address them, and view tensions and conflicts as opportunities to learn and adapt. Exchange perspectives with others along the way.

  • Start small but act with purpose. Choose wisely when to act about these challenges and when not to, based on your role and resources. Acting includes both small steps, like building connections, but also refraining from action or even doing nothing for now. Transformation efforts also require caring for yourself and your loved ones, so you are ready to act when it matters most.

  • Every result holds a lesson. Reflect on your learning journey, including both process and results for yourself, others, society, and the environment. Consider feedback (e.g. reactions, ideas, beliefs) to support your well-being and personal growth.
