By following the metaphor of the <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">“City as a Laboratory”</span>, this Workbook invites you to see cities not simply as a place that grows <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">“on its own”</span>. Instead, this module encourages you to imagine cities as shared places and projects where everyone not just a few can take part, make their own choices, and influence what happens by sharing their ideas, hopes and skills ([@saunders2010] ;  [Ward 2019](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvw04c4q?turn_away=true)).

In the imaginary view of [](#fig_2.1), the term <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">"city"</span> is not about size or location, but about places where people try to live their own lives together with others in fair and supportive conditions while learning from each other [@philippjende2025]. In such imaginary, being part of a place means more than just living there; it means being part of the community. Research before industrialisation showed that to <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">“dwell”</span> in English (“habiter” in French) meant more than simply <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">“to live”</span> or <span style="color:#0089A6; font-weight:700;">“to reside”</span> in a place. Instead, it implies an active social life within a community or town and an ongoing relationship with one’s environment. It refers to how people shape places and are shaped by them [@henri1991production]. However, consumer lifestyles and entertainment industries have often turned cities into spaces focused on commercialised public areas and high levels of consumption [@wilson2023reframing]. While this development supports economic growth, it also harms the environment through excessive resource use and high levels of emissions and waste such as food, energy, and water and affects well-being by increasing stress, overwork, inactivity, individualism, and social isolation  [@kronenberg2024].

The gap between [](#fig_2.1)imaginary and the current situation in the place where you live, study, or work reveals several types or clusters of challenges. @cities_lab_table outlines these clusters as defined on the  [2026 City Bootcamp](https://www.tu.berlin/en/vp-sl/transdisciplinary-learning/calts-the-city-as-a-laboratory/summer-school-milan-2026) . Think about a real-life example, if possible, an interconnected social, economic, and environmental challenge. Feel free to choose a challenge that you want to get familiar with or a challenge that you have worked on as part of a student, research, professional, or community project to revisit it from the Workbook's perspective. The goal is to go through the chosen example through the guidance questions of one of the clusters below.

| **Shared spaces and collective practices: Who creates and governs these in the future?** | Shared spaces, for example, in contemporary cities are increasingly shaped by tensions between shared resources and privatisation. At the same time, new forms of cooperation, mutual aid, and collective stewardship are emerging, redefining shared spaces and collective practices. | - How can new shared spaces and collective practices be created in increasingly privatised spaces?<br>- What forms of governance can involve citizens, local organisations and non-human actors?<br>- How can collective care and shared maintenance practices be fostered?<br>- How can abandoned or underused spaces be transformed into such new spaces? |
| **Justice and care: Who has access to wellbeing?** | The opportunities that shared spaces, for example, in a city offer are not equally distributed. Access to health, green space, mobility, safety, and environmental quality varies widely across neighbourhoods and social groups. | - How can territorial inequalities be reduced?<br>- How can the territory or service provided be designed around care rather than productivity alone?<br>- How can overlooked needs and experiences be made visible?<br>- How can truly inclusive environments be created for those who are more likely to be affected or harmed? |
| **Vulnerability and climate adaptation: How can we live with a changing climate?** | Places, in for example, cities will face increasing exposure to extreme weather events, resource scarcity and biodiversity loss. Adaptation is not only a technical challenge but also requires new ways of inhabiting and relating to an environment. | - How can one respond to extreme weather and new forms of vulnerability or exposure to harm or risk?<br>- How can disrupted ecological relationships be restored?<br>- How can natural and built systems be better integrated?<br>- How can territories address the challenge without increasing inequalities? |
| **Memory and conflict: Whose stories shape transformation?** | Regeneration is the process of improving and transforming parts of a place to make them stable locations to live and work. However, it often creates tensions between memories, identities, interests, and different visions of the future, where some stories are highlighted while others are overlooked or erased. | - How can marginalised histories be recognised and valued?<br>- How can conflicts between long-term residents and newcomers be addressed?<br>- How can development avoid pushing aside people's culture or stories?<br>- How can conflict support transformation? |
| **The right to transform: Who has the power to shape the future of a place?** | Urban transformation is often driven by a limited number of government and economic actors. Yet emerging forms of participation, activism and civic engagement are reshaping who has the power to envision, influence, and shape the future of cities. | - Who is excluded from decisions about the future of a place?<br>- How can participation be broadened and made more meaningful?<br>- Which non-human actors should have a voice in decision-making?<br>- What new forms of democracy could emerge? |
